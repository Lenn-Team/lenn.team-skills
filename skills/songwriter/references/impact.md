# Impact: what research says about catchy songs

This file translates findings from music psychology and practice into writing rules. Important: these are **tendencies from correlational studies**, not formulas for success. Hits come from song, performer, reach and chance. The rules raise the chance that a song sticks – they guarantee nothing.

## 1. Earworms: familiar with one distinctive deviation
**Finding:** Jakubowski et al. (2017, *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*) compared the 100 most frequently named earworms from a survey of about 3,000 people with equally successful non-earworms. Earworms tended to be faster, had a common, easy-to-remember melodic contour (often first phrase rising, second falling) and at the same time unusual intervals – leaps or conspicuous note repetitions. Radio exposure added to the likelihood.

**For the lyrics:**
- Hook line short, rhythmically distinct and easy to repeat.
- One single peculiarity per hook (unusual word, syllable repetition, rhythmic break) – not three.
- Don't damp the energy in the style prompt when catchiness is the goal; pick the tempo towards the upper end of the genre range.

## 2. Repetition: yes, but dosed
**Finding:** Nunes, Ordanini & Valsesia (2015, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*) analysed Billboard Hot 100 songs from 1958 to 2012. More chorus repetitions went with a higher chance of reaching number 1 and a faster rise; lab experiments showed that repeated lyrics are processed more easily (processing fluency). Too much word repetition, however, worked against the effect.

**For the lyrics:**
- Repeat the chorus identically (at least three times in the song), with the title in it several times.
- Concentrate repetition in the chorus, keep the verses varied.
- No endless loop of the same word without a musical reason.

## 3. Expectation and surprise
**Finding:** Cheung et al. (2019, *Current Biology*) modelled 80,000 chords from 745 Billboard songs. Experienced as pleasant: surprise when expectation was certain – or confirmation when expectation was uncertain. Amygdala, hippocampus and auditory cortex were involved.

**For the lyrics (by analogy):**
- In very predictable passages (chorus repetition, clear rhyme scheme) one line may surprise: different image, unexpected rhyme word, punchline.
- In open, unsettled passages (bridge, free verse) a clear, expected resolution feels satisfying.
- The last chorus can carry a small variation (one changed word, a new meaning through the bridge).

## 4. Attention in streaming
**Finding:** Léveillé Gauvin (2018, *Musicae Scientiae*) studied US top-10 singles 1986–2015: instrumental intros shrank from about 20 to about 5 seconds, the title came earlier, titles got shorter, tempo rose. In a second test with Spotify data (most vs. least streamed songs by the same artists) he did not find this effect, however.

Platform logic: Spotify counts a stream only after 30 seconds of listening. Short-video platforms cut 15–30 second excerpts.

**For songs aiming for reach:**
- Vocals within the first ~10 seconds, short intro (`[Short Intro]` or straight into `[Verse 1]`).
- Title or hook motif within the first 30–45 seconds (e.g. via a short pre-chorus or a hook preview in the intro).
- Plan a 15–30 second excerpt that works on its own (usually chorus or post-chorus) – "clip-ready".
- Prefer short, memorable titles; the title should be what listeners would search for.
- For album tracks, ballads and genres with their own logic (indie, prog, ambient, classical crossover) these points apply only loosely.

## 5. Songwriting practice
- **Prosody** (e.g. Pat Pattison, Berklee): form and content should match – express stability with stable forms (equal line lengths, perfect rhyme, resolution on a strong beat), unrest with unstable ones (odd number of lines, near rhyme, ending on a weak beat).
- **Verse/chorus contrast:** verses in a lower range, more syllables, shorter note values; chorus higher, fewer syllables, longer notes. In the lyrics: verse dense and narrative, chorus short and open.
- **Mirror syllable patterns:** the Scandinavian pop school (described e.g. in John Seabrook's *The Song Machine*) keeps syllable count and rhythm of corresponding lines deliberately identical ("melodic math") so melodic phrases can repeat.
- **Get to the chorus:** the old radio rule "Don't bore us, get to the chorus" matches point 4.
- **The specific is universal:** a specific detail (the mug with the chip) creates more identification than a general statement (the good times), because listeners insert their own memories.

## 6. Put it into the brief
Record under "Impact" in the song brief:
- Hook idea and its one peculiarity
- Time of vocal entry / title / first chorus
- Clip excerpt (which section, approx. seconds)
- Where the surprise sits (line, section)
- Repetition plan (how often the chorus, variation in the last one)
