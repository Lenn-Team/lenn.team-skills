# German

Rules for writing German lyrics. Examples stay in German; explanations to a German-speaking user are given in German.

## 1. Stress
- The main stress of multisyllabic words stays where it falls in speech (usually on the stem syllable).
- Inseparable prefixes (*be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-, ent-, emp-, miss-*) are unstressed: ver-GES-sen, er-ZÄH-len.
- Separable verb particles (*an-, auf-, aus-, ab-, mit-, zu-, vor-, nach-*) carry the stress: AUF-stehen, AN-rufen.
- One-syllable words get their weight from the sentence. A stressed preposition or article shifts the meaning ("ICH hab das gesagt" ≠ "ich hab DAS gesagt") – use only deliberately.
- Check loanwords, names and compounds with unclear stress (*Montag* vs. *Montage*).

## 2. Counting syllables
- Count sung syllables, not letters. Diphthongs (*ei, ai, au, eu, äu*) are one syllable nucleus; *ie* is a long i.
- Syllabic consonants: "haben" is often sung "ham", "gehen" → "gehn". Fix **one** version per line.
- Colloquial contractions change the count: "ich habe" (3) / "ich hab" (2), "gibt es" (3) / "gibt's" (2). Apostrophe only where standard (Duden): gibt's, wie geht's; "hab", "mach", "find" without apostrophe.
- Same syllable count ≠ same stress pattern. With a melody also check upbeats, long notes and phrase endings.

## 3. Rhymes
- Perfect rhyme from the last stressed vowel including the following sounds: *Zeit/weit*, *bleiben/schreiben*, *still/will*.
- Vowel length matters: *schön/schon* is not a perfect rhyme; *Stadt/hat* is (both short, same ending), *Staat/hat* is not.
- Feminine rhymes (*bleiben/schreiben*) need room for the unstressed final syllable; masculine rhymes (*Zeit/weit*) close phrases harder.
- Assonance and internal rhyme are common in German pop and rap and sound more modern than couplets.

## 4. Syntax before rhyme
- In a neutral main clause the finite verb is in second position. Moving verbs to the line end just to rhyme ("ich dich im Herzen immer trage") sounds like a Schlager parody.
- Avoid genitive chains and nominal style ("die Sehnsucht der Vergänglichkeit der Zeit"); break up compounds at fast tempos.
- Fragments, ellipses and repetitions are allowed. A random mix of officialese, youth slang and pathos is not.
- Stay in the chosen register: "krass" and "fürwahr" don't belong in the same song.

## 5. Sound and singability
- Avoid consonant clusters on fast syllables (*"du sprichst's"*, *"Herbststurm"*).
- Prefer open vowels (a, o, ei, au) on long notes; *ü* and *i* are harder in high registers.
- Final devoicing and *-ch* at the end of a phrase sound hard – use deliberately.
- Breathing points at sense boundaries.

## 6. Register by genre
- **Deutschpop/indie:** conversational, precise perspective, own images.
- **Schlager:** direct address, perfect rhyme, clear repetition, familiar phrases with one original punchline.
- **Deutschrap:** flow, multisyllabic and internal rhymes, punchlines; anglicisms and slang only if they fit the character.
- **Liedermacher/folk:** narrative, wordplay, punchline.
- **Ballad:** restraint, space, few strong images.
- **Dialect:** only on request and then consistently; agree on spelling with the user; test generator pronunciation.

## 7. German in Suno and ElevenLabs
Experience values, test case by case:
- Write out abbreviations as spoken: "DRK" → "De-Er-Ka", "THW" → "Te-Ha-We".
- Write out numbers: "1998" → "neunzehnhundertachtundneunzig".
- Spell anglicisms and names phonetically where needed; test unusual place names.
- English mispronunciation of German words (e.g. "Rat" sung like English "rat"): defuse problem words with hyphens or phonetic spelling ("Raat").
- Name "German lyrics"/"German vocals" in the style prompt if the generator drifts into English.
- Sing-along lyrics always in correct spelling; phonetic spelling only in the generator version.
