# English

## 1. Stress and rhythm
- English is stress-timed: stressed syllables come at roughly equal intervals, unstressed ones get compressed. Lines with different syllable counts can therefore sound equally long – stresses count more than syllables.
- Check word stress, especially for words whose stress changes with the word class (*REcord* vs. *reCORD*, *PREsent* vs. *preSENT*).
- Function words (*the, a, of, to, and*) not on strong beats.

## 2. Rhyme
- English pop works a lot with near rhymes (*time/mind*, *home/alone*), assonance and consonance – more natural than forced perfect rhymes.
- Avoid worn-out pairs (`../anti-ai.md`).
- Rap: multisyllabic rhymes, internal rhymes, rhyme chains across several lines.

## 3. Language
- No archaic inversions for the rhyme ("the love that I to you did give").
- Contractions only in standard form (*I'm, don't, they'd, wouldn't, gonna, wanna* in a fitting register). No contractions on nouns or brand names.
- Fix the register (US/UK, slang yes/no) and keep it.
- Concrete one-syllable words carry hooks better than Latinate polysyllables.

## 4. Homographs and pronunciation (generators)
Words with several pronunciations need a decision and, if needed, phonetic spelling in the generator version: *live, read, lead, wind, wound, close, tear, bass, bow, row, minute, desert, object*. Collect the decisions from the user in one batch and record them in the pronunciation table; apply them everywhere.

## 5. For non-native speakers
If the user wants English lyrics without being a native speaker:
- Use idioms only if they are really used that way – research when in doubt.
- Check for first-language syntax ("I am since three years here") and false friends (e.g. German speakers: *become*, *eventually*, *actual*).
- Better plain and correct than forcedly poetic.
